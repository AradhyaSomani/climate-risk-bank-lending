# Phase 0 D: Literature and policy context

Oct 23, 2026 · Devna Roopesh (Person B)

This is Phase 0, Section D of the Climate Risk in Indian Bank Lending plan. It covers prior work on disasters and bank lending, the RBI's climate disclosure framework, and what this project adds.

## Literature

Fourteen studies are listed here, six of them on India. The evidence points in different directions. Lending often rises after a disaster when banks are diversified or when credit is rolled over. It falls where banks are local and funding is hit, and loan rates and NPLs rise. Only one study, the Kerala floods paper, uses RBI district credit data around a single flood. No study links a district hazard measure to many events and then to a stress test.

**India**

| Paper | Country | Data | Method | Main result | Why it matters here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Beyer, Narayanan and Thakur (2022), Kerala floods](https://nipfp.org.in/publications/working-papers/1982) (NIPFP WP 383; [summary](https://www.ideasforindia.in/topics/environment/economic-dynamics-of-natural-disasters-evidence-from-the-kerala-floods)) | India (Kerala, 2018) | RBI district credit and deposits, nightlights, NPCI ATM data, CMIE household panel | Difference-in-differences, Kerala vs Karnataka and Tamil Nadu | Credit rose in Kerala relative to controls in Q4 2018, mainly for housing. ATM withdrawals and household spending fell. | The closest existing work and the Phase 3 anchor case. It covers one event, so a multi-event design is what's new here. |
| [Das, Majilla and Saurav (2022), Natural disasters, interest rates and economic activity](https://website.cafral.org.in/sites/default/files/2025-07/172023120545pm_6-tanmoy-majilla_natural_disaster_and_loan_rate_ad_tm_ss.pdf) (CAFRAL draft) | India, 2000 to 2012 | RBI BSR-1 branch-level loans (about 111,000 branches), EM-DAT/GDIS geocoded events, CMIE Prowess firms | Branch fixed effects and local projections | Loan rates rise about 62 bp over two years. Loans outstanding fall about 5% (agriculture about 7%) and recover by year four. | The nearest multi-event Indian study, but it uses restricted branch data. It tells us the size of effect to expect and argues that our public district data matters. |
| [De and Vij (2013), Bank response to rural credit demand shocks](https://www.ideasforindia.in/topics/money-finance/are-banks-responsive-to-credit-demand-shocks-in-rural-india) (ISB WP) | India, 1993 to 2010 | RBI BSR district occupation-wise credit, IMD rainfall | District panel with drought indicators | Agricultural credit rises 3% to 5% after a drought year. Most of it is fresh loans to existing borrowers, which is consistent with evergreening. | It shows that BSR district data supports this design. It's also a warning that a rise in credit after a shock can be rollover, not recovery. |
| [Boungou and Gupta (2024), Banking activities and natural disasters](https://faculty-psbedu.paris/fr/node/42850) (Applied Economics Letters) | India (2001 Gujarat earthquake) | 366 banks in 141 cities, 1995 to 2007 | Difference-in-differences | Banks in affected cities lent less, held more securities and took less risk. | This is bank-level Indian evidence of credit falling, which contrasts with Kerala. The sign of the effect is an open question. |
| [Swami (2024), Climate change and bank stability](https://ideas.repec.org/h/spr/prbchp/978-981-97-6687-1_3.html) (Springer chapter; author at RBI) | India, 2002 to 2019 | Bank panel, disaster frequency and losses, temperature | Panel regression | More frequent and costlier disasters, and higher temperatures, are linked to lower bank stability. | This is bank-level stability evidence for the asset-quality assumptions in Phase 4. It isn't geographic. |
| [Czura and Klonner (2010), The tsunami and the chit fund](https://ideas.repec.org/p/zbw/gdec10/46.html) | India (Tamil Nadu, 2004 tsunami) | Records of a semi-formal Rosca lender, tsunami geodata | Comparison of affected and unaffected branches | Rosca interest rates rose about 5.3% in affected branches, mostly in the first three months. | Informal credit absorbs part of the shock, which RBI data won't show. |

| [Brei, Mohan and Strobl (2019), Hurricane strikes in the Caribbean](https://boris.unibe.ch/129857) (QREF 72) | Eastern Caribbean | Quarterly bank data, hurricane losses | Bank panel | Deposits are withdrawn and lending falls. | [Noth and Schüwer (2017), Natural disaster and bank stability](https://safe-frankfurt.de/publications/working-papers/details/publicationname/natural-disaster-and-bank-stability-evidence-from-the-us-financial-system.html) (SAFE WP 167) | US | Weather-disaster property damage, bank balance sheets | Bank panel | Lower z-scores, higher NPA and foreclosure ratios, lower ROA and equity. The effects fade after a few years. | Supports an asset-quality channel for Phase 4, with a horizon of a few years. |
| [Schüwer, Lambert and Noth (2017), Banks and Hurricane Katrina](https://ideas.repec.org/p/zbw/safewp/94.html) (SAFE WP 94) | US (Katrina, 2005) | Banks in disaster areas by ownership and capital | Quasi-experimental comparison | Well-capitalised independent banks raised capital ratios and new lending. Their counties grew faster. | Bank capital and ownership shape the response, which feeds the bank-group view in Phase 4. |
| [Nie, Regelink and Wang (2023), Banking sector risk after disasters](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099614202282325736/pdf/IDU010b823eb0a253042430a92f0c19e5f0c80bc.pdf) (World Bank PRWP 10326) | 184 countries, 1980 to 2019; Philippines regions | EM-DAT, FinStats, IMF FSIs, BSP regional NPLs | Local projections | Severe disasters raise system NPL ratios by 0.37 to 0.61 pp, with larger effects in South Asia. In the Philippines, 1 pp more typhoon damage to GDP raises NPLs about 0.72%. | An external calibration range for the NPL assumption in Phase 4, since RBI district data has no defaults. |

## RBI climate disclosure framework

The framework is still a draft and has been put on hold. No final version had been issued as of the latest report found, from 30 January 2026.

| Date | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 30 Jan 2026 | Reports say the RBI deferred plans to mandate climate-risk disclosure by banks, with no new date. Officials called it "not a priority at this point of time" and cited compliance cost and a mismatch with SEBI rules ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/finance/news/rbi-likely-defers-plan-to-mandate-climate-risk-disclosures-by-banks-126012900785_1.html), [Angel One](https://www.angelone.in/news/market-updates/rbi-postpones-mandatory-climate-risk-reporting-for-banks)). |
| 28 Nov 2025 | The RBI issued final Climate Finance and Management of Climate Change Risks Directions, 2025, for commercial banks. These cover green deposits, green finance and greenwashing. They do not replace the disclosure draft ([TeamLease RegTech](https://teamleaseregtech.com/updates/article/49972/reserve-bank-of-india-commercial-banks-climate-finance-and-management-of-climate-change-risks-directions-2025/), [Uniqus](https://uniqus.com/building-credibility-in-green-finance/)). |
| 18 Jul 2025 | Reuters, via Deccan Herald, reported that rules covering stress tests and emissions reporting were due "in few months" and by FY27 ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/business/rbi-to-issue-climate-risk-disclosure-rules-for-banks-in-few-months-report-3636590)). |
| 28 Feb 2024 | The RBI released the [Draft Disclosure framework on Climate-related Financial Risks, 2024](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/bs_viewcontent.aspx?Id=4393). It applies to SCBs (excluding LABs, payments banks and RRBs), Tier-IV UCBs, AIFIs and top and upper layer NBFCs. It has four TCFD pillars. Governance, strategy and risk management would apply from FY 2025-26, and metrics and targets from FY 2027-28. Strategy disclosures ask whether climate risk feeds into ICAAP and climate scenario analysis. |

What this means for the project: banks are not yet required to publish district-level physical-risk exposure. A transparent research stress test of public data fills a real gap. Before the Phase 0 gate, check the RBI site once more for a final framework issued after January 2026.

## What this project adds

The Kerala study covers one flood in one state with neighbouring states as controls. Das, Majilla and Saurav cover many events, but they rely on restricted branch-level data and study loan pricing. The international work shows that the sign of the lending response depends on bank structure, and it does not use Indian data. This project adds three things. The first is a consistent district flood and cyclone hazard score linked to public RBI district credit and deposits over two decades. The second is a multi-event staggered design that reports effects on credit and deposits by bank group, with spillover-aware controls and honest intervals. The third is a transparent stress test whose every assumption is an input, with the asset-quality step calibrated from outside estimates such as Nie et al. (2023), since RBI district data has no defaults. All of it can be rebuilt from public data at a time when the RBI's own disclosure rules remain on hold.
